**PROTOCOL TITLE:**

Response: Comparative Analysis of Large Language Models and Human Reviewers in Grant Application Evaluation: A Study of the School of Health Professions Research Seed Grant Program

**SHORT TITLE/ACRONYM:**

Response: COMPARE-LLM (Comparative Performance Assessment of Reviewers - Large Language Models)

**LAY DESCRIPTION:**

Response: This study compares how well artificial intelligence computer programs can review and score research grant applications compared to human experts. We will test whether AI systems can provide consistent and fair evaluations of grant proposals by analyzing the same applications that were previously reviewed in our university's seed grant program. No personal information will be used, and the goal is to understand if AI could help make grant review processes more efficient and unbiased.

**1.0 Objectives**

_1.1 Describe the purpose of this research. Explain why it is important to do the study. If there are hypothesis, please list them here._

Response: The purpose of this research is to evaluate and compare the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) against human reviewers in assessing grant applications for the School of Health Professions Research Seed Grant Program. This study aims to determine whether LLMs can provide consistent, reliable, and unbiased evaluation of grant proposals using structured scoring criteria.

Hypotheses:
1. LLMs will demonstrate comparable scoring consistency across multiple review iterations compared to human reviewers
2. LLMs will show less inter-reviewer variability in scoring patterns than human reviewers
3. LLMs will provide structured, criterion-based feedback that aligns with established grant review standards

This research is important because it could inform future grant review processes, potentially reducing reviewer burden while maintaining evaluation quality and reducing bias in the review process.

**2.0 Background**

_2.1 Provide the scientific or scholarly background, rationale, and significance of the research based on the existing literature and how it will contribute/fill in gaps to existing knowledge. Include complete citations or references:_

Response:

Grant peer‐review is central to allocating scarce research funds, yet decades of work show substantial inter-reviewer disagreement and vulnerability to bias, which can affect fairness and reproducibility in funding decisions (Pier et al., 2018; Hesselberg et al., 2025). Reviewer burden is also non-trivial: estimates suggest peer review consumes millions of hours of researcher time annually and represents a large implicit economic cost to the research enterprise (Tennant et al., 2020; Aczel et al., 2021). Recent qualitative work further highlights threats such as workload pressures and process variability that can erode consistency and perceived legitimacy of grant evaluations (Gould et al., 2025; Oxley & Bacevic, 2025). These challenges motivate exploration of scalable, assistive technologies that could improve consistency and transparency while reducing burden.

Large Language Models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly in structured text evaluation and summarization tasks and are being assessed across research-adjacent workflows, including manuscript screening and elements of peer review (Lee, 2025; Saleh et al., 2025; Shool et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2025). Early studies suggest that, under carefully designed prompts and guardrails, LLMs can approximate some human decisions in screening titles/abstracts and generating review primitives, offering potential gains in speed and standardization (Rubinstein et al., 2025; Homiar et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2025). At the same time, known limitations—including domain-specific validity gaps, over-generalization, and variable faithfulness—warrant rigorous, task-specific evaluation before operational use in high-stakes settings (Uwe & Chin-Yee, 2025; Lee, 2025).

Against this backdrop, there remains a clear gap: the comparative performance of LLMs versus human reviewers on full grant applications evaluated against institutionally defined criteria has not been systematically quantified. This study addresses that gap by comparing LLM and human evaluations of previously reviewed, de-identified seed-grant applications using harmonized scoring rubrics, with a focus on agreement, reliability, and criterion-aligned feedback quality. Because the study analyzes de-identified, previously collected documents and does not involve intervention or interaction with individuals, it is well-positioned to generate empirical evidence under a minimal-risk, secondary-research framework (45 CFR 46.104\[d]\[4]) while informing pragmatic considerations for future hybrid human-AI review processes (HHS/OHRP, 2025; eCFR, 2025).

**9.0 Data Management and Analysis**

_9.1 Describe the data analysis plan, including any statistical procedures. This section applies to both quantitative and qualitative analysis._

Response: The analysis will use Python statistical libraries (pandas, scipy.stats, statsmodels) to conduct descriptive statistics, inter-rater reliability analysis using intraclass correlation coefficients, and ANOVA tests to compare scoring patterns across LLM models and review criteria. Qualitative analysis of LLM feedback will employ natural language processing techniques using NLTK and scikit-learn libraries for content analysis and topic modeling. All data will be stored in SQLite databases and analyzed through Jupyter notebooks with appropriate statistical tests and significance levels set at α = 0.05.
